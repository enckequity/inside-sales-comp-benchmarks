The one-page case

Each closer generates about $2 million in gross profit a year. Let's align how we pay them with the value they help create.

Today a closer earns a $75,000 base plus a 0.06% commission — about $80,760 a year. This brief lays out a base-plus-per-unit plan that brings pay closer to how comparable high-ticket dealers structure it: a solid closer near $195k–$225k, paid only on units actually delivered, with the company still keeping the large majority of every unit's gross.

Section VI

Questions leadership will reasonably ask

Do we really know our margins well enough to do this?
We do — they're on every build sheet (26.55% base, 36.95% options). And the plan pays a flat per-unit amount, so it doesn't depend on margin precision: it stays affordable whether the real margin is 18%, 22%, or 27% (Section V). A flat per-unit structure is actually the safest way to handle any margin uncertainty.
Isn't $75k already solid pay?
It genuinely is. The narrower question this brief raises is whether it reflects the unusually high gross our closers generate (~$2M each), and what it would take to retain them. A competing dealer could offer $150k and still be getting a strong return on that production — so this is really about staying competitive for the people who move our inventory.
Leads are inbound, and lead flow is expensive — does that change it?
It's a fair point, and it's built into the proposal: because leads are company-generated, the plan pays a closing rate (~5–6%), not a hunter's rate. And since lead flow is a real investment, keeping a proven closer protects the return on it — a strong closer converts more of those paid leads and holds margin. Retention and lead ROI point the same direction.
