# Objections, Answered

Every pushback leadership will raise, met with leadership's own numbers. Read this before the meeting.

---

## 1. "We don't really know our margins."

**We do — and we just proved it.** Here is a real Pine Hill build sheet:

| | Price | Cost | Gross | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base unit | $76,240 | $56,000 | $20,240 | **26.55%** |
| Factory options | $10,150 | $6,400 | $3,750 | **36.95%** |
| **Total** | **$86,390** | **$62,400** | **$23,990** | **27.8%** |

Those percentages come straight out of our pricing/costing system. We know the margin on every unit we quote — it's printed on the worksheet.

**But here's the part that ends the objection:** the proposed plan is a **flat dollar amount per unit**, not a percentage of margin. It pays **$1,000–$1,250 per delivered unit** regardless of the exact margin. So even if margins were a total mystery, the plan would still be payable, predictable, and capped at a known dollar figure per sale.

And it's affordable across any margin we might actually have:

| If real margin is… | Gross/unit ($80k avg) | Company keeps after $1,000 commission |
|---|---|---|
| 18% (pessimistic) | $14,400 | $13,400 (93%) |
| 22% | $17,600 | $16,600 (94%) |
| 27% (our build sheets) | $21,600 | $20,600 (95%) |

We keep **93–95% of the gross on every unit** after paying the closer. "We don't know our margins" is, if anything, an argument *for* a flat per-unit plan — and for tightening our margin reporting — not an argument for underpaying the people who generate the margin.

---

## 2. "You already make a healthy $75k salary."

$75k is a healthy salary for an **average job.** It is not a healthy number for someone who personally generates **~$9.6M in revenue and ~$2M in gross profit a year.** Those are two completely different questions, and conflating them is the mistake.

The honest yardstick isn't *"is $75k good pay?"* — it's:

- **What is $75k as a share of what the rep produces?** ~3–4% of the gross. The dealer-industry norm for a salesperson's total comp is **20–35% of the gross they produce** ([source](https://proactivetrainingsolutions.com/dealership-pay-plan-design-compensation/)). We're paying roughly **one-sixth to one-ninth** of the benchmark.
- **What will it cost to replace this person?** A competing dealer can offer **$150k — double our number — and still be getting a steal** on a $2M-gross producer. Every month that gap exists, we're carrying retention risk on $9.6M of revenue.

We would never benchmark our parts manager's pay against the owner's, or a lot porter's against a closer's. Benchmarking a $10M producer against an "average salary" is the same category error. → [What this role actually pays elsewhere](market-comparison.md)

## 3. "It's all inbound and we pay a lot for lead flow."

**True — and it's the single best argument for paying the closer well, not against it.**

Think about where the money is made. We spend heavily to generate a lead. That lead is worth **nothing** until someone converts it. The closer is the exact point where our expensive lead spend either turns into ~$22k of gross or gets wasted. On identical inbound leads:

- A **strong closer** converts more of them, holds margin (less discounting), and attaches more high-margin options.
- A **mediocre closer** takes the base order, caves on price, and lets a chunk of paid-for leads walk.

The difference between a 30% and a 38% close rate on the *same* leads is enormous gross — and it's entirely down to who's on the phone. **Paying a competitive commission is how we protect the ROI on every lead dollar we already spend.**

Three more points that close this objection:

1. **We're already pricing in the inbound reality.** Because leads are company-generated, the plan pays a **closing rate (~5–6% of gross), not a hunter's rate (15–25%).** That's why the number is $195k, not $400k. The objection is already baked into the proposal.
2. **Pay on gross, and incentives align perfectly.** A commissioned closer is motivated to do exactly what protects lead ROI: convert, hold price, upsell the 37%-margin options. Salary motivates none of that.
3. **The real risk runs the other way.** Underpay the closer → they leave → our expensive lead flow now pours into a green rep at a lower close rate. We don't save money; we **light the lead budget on fire** while the gross drops. The comp "savings" is a rounding error next to the lead spend we'd be wasting.

**Cost-of-sale, fully loaded:** even adding heavy lead cost on top of the proposed comp, total selling cost stays under the 20–35% industry benchmark — and the *comp* portion specifically is the underweight piece. Shifting a few points toward the human who closes the leads is higher-ROI than buying more leads for a weaker closer to fumble. → [Cost-of-sale model](economics.md#cost-of-sale)

---

## 4. "Can we afford it?" (the quiet objection)

It's **100% variable and self-funding.** The commission is only paid on a unit that's *already been sold and delivered* — i.e., the ~$22k of gross is already in the building before we pay the $1,000. On units we don't sell, the plan costs **$0.** There is no fixed-cost increase, no exposure in a slow month. It's the safest possible kind of raise: pay-for-performance, funded by the performance.

And it likely **grows the pie:** rewarding option attach (36.95% margin) means more gross per deal — part or all of the commission can come from gross the plan itself creates.

---

## 5. "Won't every rep want this / won't it set a precedent?"

Yes — and that's the point. A transparent, generous-but-below-market closing plan is a **recruiting and retention magnet** for exactly the people who move $10M of product a year. The "precedent" we'd be setting is: *Pine Hill pays its producers like producers.* That's a competitive advantage, not a liability.
